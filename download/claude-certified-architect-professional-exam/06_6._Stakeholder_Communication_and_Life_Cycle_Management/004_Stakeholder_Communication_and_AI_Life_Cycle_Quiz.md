## Chapter summary[#](https://www.educative.io/courses/claude-certified-architect-professional-exam/stakeholder-communication-and-life-cycle-quiz#Chapter-summary)

Before moving on, make sure these ideas are solid.

1. Discovery separates what a stakeholder describes wanting from what they actually need. A direct set of questions, what happens without this, what does success look like, who is affected and how, surfaces the real target a stated request often obscures.
2. A requirement states an outcome, and a constraint states a limit, and conflating the two is costly. A stated integration, platform, or vendor is usually a constraint rather than a requirement, and the two categories are negotiated very differently.
3. A stated limit is only binding if the answer to “what happens if we miss it” confirms that. A preference dressed up as a constraint has more flexibility than it first appears to have, and only the direct test tells the two apart.
4. The same decision needs a different framing for a technical audience and an executive audience. Mechanism serves one; cost, risk, and business outcome serve the other, and neither framing changes what was actually decided.
5. A trade-off communicated honestly, and feedback routed to a real answer, both protect the same thing: an agreement that survives contact with reality. A named cost cannot ambush a stakeholder later, and feedback checked against what it actually touches changes a decision only when it should.
6. Documentation is a thread through the whole lifecycle rather than a deliverable produced once at handoff. It has to capture why a decision was made, translate guardrail and alert behavior into action a team can take without the architect, and carry an owner and a trigger so it never quietly stops being true.
