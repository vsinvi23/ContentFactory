# The OpenAI FDE Interview Loop

The OpenAI FDE interview process emphasizes understanding candidates' problem-solving approaches, decision-making, and communication skills. Preparation should align with OpenAI's guidelines, which highlight the importance of collaboration and adaptability. Candidates are advised to practice both with and without AI tools, as expectations vary by interview format. Reports from previous candidates can provide insights into the process, but they should not be taken as definitive. Effective communication and a clear demonstration of results are crucial throughout the interview stages.

A candidate spent three weekends preparing for what they expected to be a five-hour take-home assignment. Based on several online accounts of OpenAI FDE interviews, they practised building projects with an AI coding assistant available throughout. The recruiter's email then confirmed a different format: a live pair-programming session where AI assistance was not allowed.

The practice was still useful, but it had not prepared the candidate for the constraints of the actual interview. They had prepared for the interview they had read about, not the one they were invited to.

A more reliable approach is to use OpenAI's official guidance as a starting point, treat candidate reports as supplementary information, and adapt preparation once the recruiter confirms the interview format and permitted tools. This lesson outlines the interview process using that approach. The next five lessons examine each stage in detail.

## What does OpenAI’s interview guide say?[#](https://www.educative.io/courses/forward-deployed-engineer/the-openai-fde-interview-loop#What-does-OpenAIs-interview-guide-say)

OpenAI publishes an [interview guide](https://openai.com/interview-guide/) that describes an example process and says plainly that individual experiences may differ. It is not specific to the FDE role.

|  |  |
| --- | --- |
| **Stage** | **What the Guide Describes** |
| Application and résumé review | The recruiting team typically replies within a week |
| Introductory calls | A conversation with the hiring manager or a recruiter about experience, motivations, and goals |
| Skills-based assessment | Formats vary by team and may include pair coding, take-home projects, or technical tests; some roles have more than one; the recruiting team provides prep |
| Final interviews | Virtual by default, with an option to interview onsite in San Francisco; typically 4–6 hours with 4–6 people over 1–2 days, focused on the candidate’s area of expertise |
| Decision | Typically within a week of the final interviews; the recruiter may ask for references |

The guide says interviews are designed to understand how candidates approach problems, make decisions, and communicate their reasoning. For engineering interviews, OpenAI generally looks for well-designed solutions, high-quality code, optimal performance, and good test coverage. Across all interviews, it evaluates communication and collaboration.

The guide’s hiring philosophy adds two useful points. OpenAI says it is not credential-driven and wants to understand each candidate’s background and contribution. And it looks for experts and for people with “high potential,” which it defines as having shown the ability to ramp up quickly in a new domain and produce results. Collaboration, effective communication, openness to feedback, and alignment with OpenAI’s mission and values come up throughout.

For preparation, the guide recommends the OpenAI Charter, research publications, and blog posts, and suggests getting familiar with recent updates related to the team being interviewed for.

## What do FDE candidates report?[#](https://www.educative.io/courses/forward-deployed-engineer/the-openai-fde-interview-loop#What-do-FDE-candidates-report)

Because the guide is general, candidates look for FDE-specific accounts. Several public write-ups and secondary interview guides describe an FDE loop along these lines. One that collects them is [The Forward Deployed’s OpenAI page](https://www.theforwarddeployed.io/interviews/openai), reviewed on July 13, 2026, which itself notes that OpenAI doesn’t publish its FDE loop. These are candidate reports, not OpenAI’s published process, and they disagree with each other in the details.

|  |  |
| --- | --- |
| **Reported stage** | **What Candidates Describe** |
| Recruiter screen | A short call, often described as about 30 minutes, on background, deployment experience, and motivation |
| Take-home | An open-ended customer problem built with OpenAI’s APIs, often described as a few hours of work, sometimes with a recorded walkthrough |
| Technical session | A live discussion that starts from the take-home and extends into LLM systems: retrieval, evals, failure modes, cost |
| Final interviews | Several sessions that may include solution design for a new scenario, technical depth, and a hiring manager conversation |

Every row maps onto a category in OpenAI’s guide: introductory calls, skills-based assessments, and final interviews. That is the safe way to use reports. They suggest what to practice. They never tell us the order, the duration, or the scoring of our own loop, and a second website repeating the same account is still one account.

## Which set of instructions win?[#](https://www.educative.io/courses/forward-deployed-engineer/the-openai-fde-interview-loop#Which-set-of-instructions-win)

Candidates may receive information from three sources before an assessment, but these sources carry different levels of authority.

1. **Assessment instructions:** Assessment-specific materials and recruiter guidance determine the format, requirements, and permitted tools.
2. **OpenAI's interview guide:** The official guide outlines a typical interview process, which may vary by role.
3. **Candidate reports:** These offer examples of possible interview formats and questions but may not reflect the current process.

This distinction is especially important when deciding which tools are permitted. OpenAI's interview guide states that AI tool permissions vary by assessment. Some formats allow AI assistance, while others evaluate independent problem-solving without it. The preparation materials specify which tools are permitted. If the instructions are unclear, candidates should confirm with their recruiter before the assessment.

Another candidate's experience, or OpenAI's development of AI coding tools, does not establish permission to use those tools during a specific assessment.

A practical approach is to prepare for both assisted and independent coding. Practise solving problems both with an AI coding assistant and independently, so you can adapt to either assessment format.

## What does each stage look for?[#](https://www.educative.io/courses/forward-deployed-engineer/the-openai-fde-interview-loop#What-does-each-stage-look-for)

OpenAI doesn’t publish an FDE scorecard. What we can do is read the role description from this chapter’s first lesson against each stage and derive preparation targets. These are targets for practice, not a rubric anyone at OpenAI uses.

|  |  |  |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Stage** | **What to Show** | **Lesson** |
| Introductory call | A real reason for OpenAI and for FDE work, and one project told clearly | 9 |
| Coding | A small, practical solution with explicit checks, tests for failure cases, and clear reasoning out loud | 10 |
| Take-home or case presentation | Scoping an ambiguous request, choosing a first release, naming its proof and its owner | 11 |
| Technical session on LLM systems | Defending retrieval, evals, latency, cost, and failure handling down to the mechanism | 12 |
| Hiring manager conversation | Ownership, customer judgment, and delivery under ambiguity | 9 |
| Final interviews | The same facts told consistently to several people, including on mission and safety | 13 |

Communication and judgment run through every row. A strong retrieval design still needs an answer to “who may see the retrieved material, and what happens when the access check fails?”

## How should we practice?[#](https://www.educative.io/courses/forward-deployed-engineer/the-openai-fde-interview-loop#How-should-we-practice)

One habit separates strong answers at every stage: keeping a result attached to what it actually proves. Here is the D-04 fix from the evals lesson as a practice question.

**Interviewer:** “You found the D-04 bug. What did your fix accomplish?”

**Weak answer:** “The repaired mapper passes 8 of 8 tests, so the fix works in production, and the trace shows no side effects.”

**Strong answer:** “The mapper was dropping the event status and turning ‘scheduled’ into `visit_completed=true`. I changed it to pass the status and source version through, and added an acceptance check that rejects any completion claim the source doesn’t support. The 8 of 8 result covers the local cases. I’d report it separately from the holdout set, and I haven’t shown anything about production yet.”

**Interviewer:** “Suppose the side-effect collector was offline during your runs. What does an empty effect record tell you?”

**Strong answer:** “Nothing either way. I’d keep that path switched off, restore the collector, verify its coverage, and rerun before proposing a pilot.”

The weak answer stretches a local result into a production claim. The strong answer says what the result covers and changes its plan when a fact changes, without inventing a problem.

### A rubric for your own practice[#](https://www.educative.io/courses/forward-deployed-engineer/the-openai-fde-interview-loop#A-rubric-for-your-own-practice)

Rate each practice answer on six dimensions, each from 0 to 2. This is a self-assessment tool for finding gaps. It is not how OpenAI scores anyone.

|  |  |  |  |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Dimension** | **0: Missing** | **1: Partial** | **2: Specific and Backed** |
| Customer framing | No user or workflow | Named but vague | The user, their problem, and what failure costs them |
| Ownership | Unclear who did what | Some separation | Your part, others’ parts, and who runs it now |
| Technical reasoning | No mechanism | Mechanism without trade-off | Where it failed, the fix, one real trade-off |
| Proof | No evidence, or overstated | Evidence without limits | What each result shows and where it stops |
| Safety | Ignored | Mentioned | The risky boundary, what you switch off, the fallback |
| Communication | Hard to follow | Followable with effort | Decision, proof, next step, in that order |

Don’t add the scores up. Fluent delivery can’t cancel an unsafe release decision, and a total hides exactly that.

Try it on the weak D-04 answer from above. Score each dimension, then compare with a reference rating.
